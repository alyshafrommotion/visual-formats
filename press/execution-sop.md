# Press Format — Implementation SOP

## Reference examples (look here first)

- **Reference examples:** They sit in this same folder, right next to this SOP.

**Whenever this SOP is triggered or referenced, open this folder and study the examples first** — how logos and pull-quotes are arranged, how many, and how the product sits inside the editorial frame.

## What this format is

An ad that borrows the visual language and authority of the press: publication logos, a pull-quote styled like a magazine headline, "as seen in," awards, or a mocked-up editorial layout. It works because third-party authority converts skepticism into trust.

**It runs in two modes:**
- **Real press (best case):** a real, named outlet's real, attributed coverage. The strongest and safest version.
- **Press-styled:** the editorial look is used deliberately with a **generic, clearly-not-real source** (an invented/generic outlet title and logo). This borrows the *feel* of press without claiming a real outlet's endorsement.

Strongest for **credibility building, category entry, higher-consideration or higher-priced products, and cold/skeptical audiences.**

The one property to protect: **a real publication's identity is never falsely attached to a quote it didn't give.** Real outlet = real, attributable coverage. If it isn't real, the source must read as generic/fictional, and any product claim inside it must still be honest and substantiable.

## Inputs

Expect a **messaging angle** (the claim the press proves), persona and awareness stage, the **press assets or the source approach**, and claim facts.

Two modes: real press (a real outlet's real, attributed coverage) is best. If there's no real coverage, you can still build the press-styled version, but use a **generic, clearly-not-real source** (an invented/generic title and logo) rather than a real publication's identity, and keep any claim inside it honest and substantiable. Never put a real outlet's name/logo on a quote it didn't give.

## Anatomy — the layers

### 1 · The pull-quote (hero)
- One editorial-styled headline quote, set large like a magazine cover line.
- In real-press mode: the single strongest, most specific line from real coverage, attributed to the real publication (and writer where it strengthens it).
- In press-styled mode: an honest, substantiable line presented under a generic/fictional source.

### 2 · The logo bar / "as seen in"
- Real-press mode: a tidy row of real publication logos or awards, 3–6, recognizable to the persona.
- Press-styled mode: a generic/invented outlet mark that reads editorial without impersonating a real publication.

### 3 · The product + editorial frame
- Product shot inside a layout that reads editorial: serif type, clean grid, magazine-like spacing. Restrained design does the "credible" signaling.

### 4 · The claim/benefit line + CTA
- One line connecting the press to what it means for the buyer, then a quiet CTA.

## Craft rules (priority order)

1. **Real outlet = real, attributable coverage; otherwise use a generic source.** With a real publication, every quote is sourced and every logo earned (no paraphrase presented as a quote). Without real coverage, use a clearly-not-real/generic source for the editorial styling instead of a real outlet's identity — and keep any product claim inside it honest.
2. **Lead with the most specific line,** not the most flattering adjective. "Cut my CPA in half in 3 weeks" beats "amazing product."
3. **Editorial restraint.** Serif, grid, whitespace — let the layout signal credibility.
4. **Recognizable proof for this persona** (real-press mode). Three logos they trust beat ten they don't.
5. **Translate the press into a benefit** in one line.
6. **Specificity is credibility.** Real numbers, real dates. No absolutes.
7. **Brand present but secondary to the proof.**
8. **Banned:** putting a *real* publication's name/logo on a quote it didn't give; real-outlet quotes with no attribution; "critically acclaimed"/"award-winning" with no named award; "designed to"; "seamless"; and any adjective standing in for a real quote. (A generic/fictional source used openly for editorial styling is allowed; a real outlet falsely credited is not.)

## Length caps

| Element | Cap |
|---|---|
| Pull-quote | 1 line, ≤14 words, attributed (real or generic source) |
| Logo/award bar | 3–6 items |
| Benefit line | ≤12 words |
| CTA | ≤6 words |

## Build steps

1. Read the brief: angle, persona, awareness stage, and whether real press exists.
2. If real press: gather assets and verify every quote, attribution, logo, and award. If not: define a generic/fictional source and confirm any claim inside it is honest.
3. Pick the single strongest, most specific quote as the hero.
4. Choose the source treatment (3–6 real logos/awards, or one generic outlet mark).
5. Write the benefit line that translates the press into what the buyer gets.
6. Claims pass: real-outlet quotes/logos/awards/numbers validated and sourced; no real outlet falsely credited; product claims honest.
7. Design handoff: editorial/serif layout, restrained grid, product inside the frame, attribution legible.
8. Deliver: hero pull-quote + source, logo/award list or generic mark, benefit line, CTA, layout note, sources/claims flags.

## QA checklist
- [ ] Real outlets on screen are real and attributable; any not-real source reads as generic, never a real publication falsely credited
- [ ] Hero quote is the most specific, not the most flattering
- [ ] 3–6 proof items (real mode); layout reads editorial and restrained
- [ ] One benefit line translates the press into buyer value
- [ ] Every real claim/number sourced; product claims honest; no absolutes
- [ ] Attribution legible at thumb size
